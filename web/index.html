When a Himalayan valley floods, the satellite sees dark pixels and can't tell water from shadow.

TRISHUL maps where the flood hit, what it broke, and which communities are cut off, and it marks the terrain it cannot see instead of guessing.

After a glacier collapse or cloudburst, teams need answers within hours, in canyons where roads are the only lifeline.

Shadow looks like water

Steep slopes hide from the radar. No return means a dark pixel, the same as a calm lake, so dry ridges get flagged as flooded.

Debris floods aren't smooth

Outburst floods carry boulders and till. Rough debris scatters radar brightly, so a water threshold misses most of the damage.

Monsoon clouds block optical

Sentinel-2 can be useless for weeks, exactly when these disasters happen.

Bridges are smaller than a pixel

A 3–8 m bridge disappears inside a 10 m pixel, yet it decides who can reach a hospital.

Binary maps hide doubt

A yes/no map gives dispatchers false confidence about which routes are open.

One pipeline, three answers, and an honest map of what it can't see.

Give it any bounding box in the Himalaya and an event date. It returns hazard, exposure and isolation from Sentinel radar, optical imagery, terrain and pre-event OpenStreetMap.

Hazard

Where did it hit?

  • Water, debris and channel scour mapped separately
  • Blindspot map for shadow and layover
  • Physics checks reject floods floating on ridgetops

Exposure

What was damaged?

  • Probability of damage for every building and 50 m road segment
  • Bridge failure estimated from flood stage, bank scour and radar loss
  • Pre-event OSM only, never post-event maps

Isolation

Who is cut off?

  • 200 correlated simulations of road failure
  • Cut-off probability with 90% intervals
  • Repair order ranked by people reconnected per km

What is new, and why it matters in a gorge.

Each piece answers one failure of the standard approach. Together they let radar, optics, terrain and road networks check one another.

Ray-marched radar geometry gate

Traces the radar line of sight across a 30 m terrain model to find shadow and layover, then sends those pixels to a blindspot map.

Standard: reads shadow as water.

EVENT-ADAPT optical-to-radar teaching

Clear-sky optical scenes teach a radar-and-terrain model what fresh debris looks like, so it can map debris under cloud.

Standard: public datasets have water labels only.

Hydraulic stage prior

Flow routing finds the river axis and a falling flood-stage profile. Height above drainage gates impossible detections.

Standard: ML predicts water high on canyon walls.

Sub-pixel bridge model

Combines deck overtopping, approach scour and radar backscatter loss into one failure probability.

Standard: a bridge is invisible at 10 m.

Correlated network percolation

Damage clusters in space, so road failures are simulated as a correlated field, not independent coin flips.

Standard: one shortest path on a binary map.

Claim ledger for every number

Reports and the copilot may only cite values in a verified ledger, in English and Nepali.

Standard: free-text summaries drift from the data.

The hardest problem: mapping debris that no dataset has labels for.

The August 2026 Trishuli event was mostly a rock-and-mud debris flood, and rough debris looks bright to radar, the opposite of water. Public flood benchmarks (Kuro Siwo, Sen1Floods11) only label open water, so no model could be trained on this signal directly.

Optical imagery can recognise fresh rock, but monsoon cloud covers most of the valley. Radar sees through cloud, but doesn't know what debris is. EVENT-ADAPT connects them inside the same event.

Limit we state openly: the student learns from optical pseudo-labels, not ground truth. We checked it with spatial block cross-validation (blocks at least 1 km apart), but debris extent is still an estimate.

What it can't do.

Built for the Multimodal AI Hackathon 2026 as an educational prototype. It is not for life-safety dispatch or evacuation orders.

  • It is post-event assessment, not early warning. Sentinel revisit is 6 to 12 days.
  • Terrain blocked on every track stays a blindspot and is not extrapolated.
  • Assets get a probability of damage, never a structural collapse verdict.
  • Isolation means road disconnection only. Settlements missing from pre-event OSM can't be modelled.
  • Debris extent relies on optical pseudo-labels.

Each component earns its place.

We added one component at a time on held-out Himalayan terrain. The ray-marched geometry gate is the biggest single drop in false alarms. Switch the metric to see each effect.

Reading it: adding the geometry gate cuts false alarms from 11.2% to 4.3%, and every later step adds about 0.04 F1. The full system reaches IoU 0.79 and F1 0.86 with 0.9% false alarms.

What these results do not show.

  • The ablation and the four-event benchmark use different test sets, so their F1 values are not directly comparable.
  • Debris extent depends on optical pseudo-labels. Spatial block validation limits leakage, but there is no ground-truth debris survey.
  • EMSR927 is one event. Agreement there supports the method but does not prove it for every valley.
  • Asset results are probabilities of damage, not engineering verdicts.
  • Isolation covers road access only, and only settlements mapped in pre-event OpenStreetMap.
  • This is an educational prototype, not for life-safety dispatch.
